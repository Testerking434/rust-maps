# Persuasion Deep — Influence, Biases, and Behavioral Economics

Everything that moves humans to decisions. Synthesizes every major school of persuasion in one place.

## Part 1 — Cialdini's 7 universal principles

From *Influence* and *Pre-Suasion* (Robert Cialdini, 40+ years of research).

### 1. Reciprocity
People feel obligated to give back after receiving. Power amplifiers:
- **Unexpected** (surprise > expected gift)
- **Personalized** (tailored > generic)
- **Significant** (feels valuable to the receiver, costs little to giver)

**Copy moves:** "Here's a free X before I ask for anything." Lead magnets, free audits, unexpected bonuses in the offer.

### 2. Commitment & Consistency
Once people commit to something (even small), they pressure themselves to stay consistent. Power amplifiers:
- **Active** (write it down > think it)
- **Public** (others saw it > private)
- **Effortful** (hard-earned > easy)
- **Voluntary** (chose it > forced)

**Copy moves:** Micro-commitments in funnels (quiz → email → small purchase → bigger purchase). "If you believe X, then you already agree Y makes sense."

### 3. Social Proof
When uncertain, people copy similar others. Power amplifiers:
- **Many** (10k users > 3 users)
- **Similar** ("people like you" > "people in general")
- **Uncertain context** (strongest when decision feels risky)

**Copy moves:** Testimonials with full name/photo/context, user counts, "most popular" badges, live purchase notifications, UGC.

### 4. Liking
People say yes to people they like. Power amplifiers:
- **Similarity** (mirrors you back)
- **Compliments** (sincere)
- **Cooperation** (fighting shared problem)
- **Physical attractiveness** (unfair but real)

**Copy moves:** "I used to be just like you…", founder story with shared pain, "we vs. them" framing.

### 5. Authority
People defer to experts and legitimate authority. Power amplifiers:
- **Titles** (Dr., PhD, Senior)
- **Clothes/symbols** (lab coat effect)
- **Track record** (numbers, years, credentials)
- **"Toxic authority"** — admit a small flaw first, builds trust on big claims

**Copy moves:** Case studies with specific numbers, credentials above the fold, "As featured in…", expert endorsements.

### 6. Scarcity
Things become more attractive when less available. Power amplifiers:
- **Recently restricted** (was available, now limited)
- **Competition** (others also want it)
- **Loss framing** > gain framing ("don't miss" > "get")

**Copy moves:** Countdown timers, "only 7 left", "closes Friday", "47 people viewing this", price increases.

⚠️ **Use only real scarcity** — fake countdowns destroy trust permanently.

### 7. Unity ("the *we* of it")
People say yes to their in-group, more than to people they merely like. Power amplifiers:
- **Kinship** (family language)
- **Place** (shared home, neighborhood)
- **Acting together** (rituals, synchronized movement)
- **Co-creation** (asked for advice, not opinions)

**Copy moves:** "For founders by founders", community/tribe language, "my people", insider lingo.

## Part 2 — The 50 most actionable cognitive biases

From Kahneman's *Thinking Fast and Slow* and behavioral economics research. The ones that actually matter for copy and decisions:

### Decision biases
1. **Anchoring** — first number seen biases all later numbers. Show $997 before $97.
2. **Loss aversion** — losses hurt 2-2.5× as much as equivalent gains feel good. Frame with "don't lose" not "gain".
3. **Endowment effect** — people overvalue what they own. Free trials → people hate giving them back.
4. **Decoy effect** — adding a clearly worse option makes the middle look better. Small $9 / Medium $29 / Large $31 — large is the decoy making medium feel like a deal.
5. **Default bias** — whatever is pre-selected wins most of the time. Opt-out > opt-in.
6. **Status quo bias** — people prefer things to stay the same. Frame change as "returning to normal".
7. **Choice paralysis** — too many options → no decision. 3-5 options > 10+ options.
8. **Hyperbolic discounting** — small reward now > bigger reward later. "Start today" > "great in 3 months".
9. **Sunk cost fallacy** — people keep investing in losing bets. Don't trigger this in your customers; leverage it ethically with progress bars.
10. **Commitment consistency** — see Cialdini #2.

### Judgment biases
11. **Availability heuristic** — easy-to-recall examples dominate thinking. Vivid stories > statistics.
12. **Representativeness** — people judge by resemblance to stereotype, ignoring base rates.
13. **Confirmation bias** — people seek info that confirms existing beliefs. Frame your product as confirming their worldview.
14. **Halo effect** — one good trait spills over. Great design → people assume great product.
15. **Fundamental attribution error** — we blame others' character, excuse ourselves with circumstance.
16. **Actor-observer asymmetry** — pair with #15 in testimonials.
17. **Self-serving bias** — people take credit for wins, blame others for losses.
18. **Dunning-Kruger** — beginners overestimate themselves. Sell to them differently than experts.
19. **Peak-end rule** — experience is remembered by peak and ending, not average. Design products with memorable peaks and endings.
20. **Recency bias** — recent events weighted too heavily.

### Social biases
21. **Bandwagon effect** — people do what others do. "Join 50,000+ founders…"
22. **In-group favoritism** — see Cialdini #7.
23. **Authority bias** — see Cialdini #5.
24. **Reactance** — when freedom is threatened, people resist. "You're free to say no" paradoxically increases yes.
25. **Reciprocity** — see Cialdini #1.
26. **Liking bias** — see Cialdini #4.
27. **False consensus** — people think others agree with them more than they do.
28. **Spotlight effect** — people think they're being watched/judged more than they are. Good for privacy objections.

### Memory and framing
29. **Framing effect** — "90% survival" > "10% mortality" for same fact.
30. **Priming** — exposure to one stimulus influences response to next. Words like "success", "achievement" prime purchase intent.
31. **Picture superiority** — images remembered 6× better than text.
32. **Von Restorff (isolation) effect** — things that stand out are remembered. One red button in sea of grey.
33. **Zeigarnik effect** — unfinished tasks occupy memory. Progress bars work because of this.
34. **Mere-exposure effect** — liking increases with familiarity. Retargeting works.
35. **Serial position** — first and last items remembered best. Put strongest points at start and end.
36. **Concreteness** — concrete beats abstract. "Saves you 43 minutes" > "saves time".

### Probability biases
37. **Neglect of probability** — people can't feel the difference between 1/1,000 and 1/1,000,000.
38. **Zero-risk bias** — people pay disproportionately for eliminating small risk entirely.
39. **Certainty effect** — 100% sure >>> 99% sure in emotional weight.
40. **Gambler's fallacy** — "due for a win" is false but emotionally real.
41. **Survivorship bias** — we see winners, forget losers. Critical when evaluating gurus.
42. **Optimism bias** — people think they're above average at everything.

### Identity and self
43. **Self-image consistency** — people act in ways consistent with self-perception. "People like you who X…"
44. **Labeling effect** — if you label someone, they tend to become that label.
45. **IKEA effect** — people value what they helped build. Co-creation increases perceived value.
46. **Endowed progress** — a task with fake early progress gets finished more. Loyalty cards stamped "2 free" already.
47. **Cognitive dissonance** — when belief and action conflict, people change belief (not action). Get action, belief follows.

### Temporal and risk
48. **Time inconsistency** — what we want long-term vs. short-term are different selves.
49. **Ambiguity aversion** — people prefer known risk over unknown. Remove ambiguity to reduce friction.
50. **Risk compensation** — safer = people take more risk to compensate.

## Part 3 — Behavioral economics moves that convert

From Thaler/Sunstein *Nudge* and Ariely *Predictably Irrational*.

1. **Default to the outcome you want.** Opt-out → opt-in conversion 80%+ vs. 40%.
2. **Make the friction path the unwanted path.** Frictionless "yes", friction on "no".
3. **Give them a reason — any reason.** Langer's copy machine experiment: "May I use the copier because I'm in a rush" worked almost as well as legit reasons. Just "because" unlocks compliance.
4. **Relative pricing.** $3.99 coffee is cheap next to $8 smoothie, expensive next to $0.99 drip.
5. **Gold/Silver/Bronze tiers.** Most pick middle. Price to make middle your target margin.
6. **Pre-commitment contracts.** "If I don't finish X by Friday, I donate $100." Works in sales onboarding.
7. **Temptation bundling.** Pair must-do with want-to-do.
8. **Social norms > individual appeals.** "9 out of 10 neighbors save energy" > "save energy".
9. **Implementation intentions.** "When X happens, I will Y." 3× more likely to act.
10. **Hot-cold empathy gap.** People underestimate how differently they'll act under stress. Design for the hot state, not cold.

## Part 4 — Blair Warren's One-Sentence Persuasion

> "People will do anything for those who encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions, and help them throw rocks at their enemies."

Every great sales page hits at least 3 of these 5. Audit your copy with this.

## Part 5 — Halbert, Schwartz, Caples — direct response truths

- **Headline does 80% of the work.** Spend 80% of time on the headline.
- **Enter the conversation already in the reader's head.** Don't start your story, join their story.
- **"Facts tell, stories sell."** Facts prove the story is real.
- **Specific numbers outperform round numbers.** "347 users" > "hundreds of users".
- **"You" is the most persuasive word. "Because" is the second.**
- **Every line must make them read the next line.** If they stop, you lose.
- **Objections must be handled before they form.** "You're probably thinking X. Here's why X is wrong."
- **The P.S. is the second-most read element after the headline.** Put your biggest promise there.

## Part 6 — Ethics

All of this is dual-use. Same techniques sell water to the thirsty and poison to the desperate. The line:

**Ethical persuasion:**
- Helps them get what they already want
- Removes friction from good decisions
- Makes true things feel true
- Would be okay if they knew exactly what you were doing

**Manipulation:**
- Makes them want what harms them
- Creates friction around good decisions for them
- Makes false things feel true
- Depends on them not knowing what you're doing

Use the first. It still converts. The second destroys trust and burns life-long customers.

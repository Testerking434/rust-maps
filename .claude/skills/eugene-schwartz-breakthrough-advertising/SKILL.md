# Eugene Schwartz — Breakthrough Advertising (Deep)

Eugene M. Schwartz wrote this book in 1966 and it has never been surpassed. Every major copywriter since — Halbert, Kennedy, Abraham, Hormozi, Brunson — builds on Schwartz. This skill distills the deeper framework beyond `schwartz-awareness-levels`: **mass desire theory, performance/mechanism claims, the 6 ways to sell, and the 36 headline formulas.**

## Part 1 — The Core Insight: Mass Desire

Schwartz's most famous line:

> **"The power, the force, the overwhelming urge to own that makes advertising work comes from the market itself, and not from the copy. Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears, and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product. This is the copywriter's task: not to create this mass desire — but to channel and direct it."**

This single idea overturns everything amateur copywriters try to do.

### The amateur's mistake
Amateur copy tries to **create** desire: "You NEED this product because it has X feature…" The reader doesn't want it, so the copy pushes harder, tries harder, gets desperate. Nothing converts.

### The pro's move
Pro copy **finds an existing mass desire** in the market and **channels it onto the product**. The reader already wants something; your job is to show that your product is the next logical step toward what they already want.

### Three attributes of mass desire

Before writing any copy, measure the mass desire you are channeling:

1. **Urgency** — how quickly does the prospect want this?
   - High urgency = immediate (pain, crisis, deadline)
   - Low urgency = "someday, maybe"
2. **Intensity** — how strongly do they want it?
   - High intensity = burning, obsessing, losing sleep
   - Low intensity = mild interest
3. **Scope** — how widely is this desire felt in the market?
   - High scope = most people feel it
   - Low scope = niche segment

**The best markets have high urgency, high intensity, and high scope.** High intensity + low scope can also work (niche premium). Low intensity + low urgency = almost impossible to sell no matter how good the copy.

### Where mass desire comes from
Mass desire is not invented by marketers. It's created by:
- Life circumstances (age, career stage, family, body)
- Social comparison (seeing others)
- Trends (cultural moments)
- Anxiety / fear of loss
- Identity aspirations
- Biology (health, mating, status, safety, belonging)

**Your job as a marketer is to listen** — talk to customers, read reviews, read reddit threads, analyze search queries — until you can state in one sentence what the prospect already wants. Then channel that.

## Part 2 — The 3 diagnostic questions (before writing a word)

Schwartz's opening ritual for every piece of copy:

### Question 1 — What is the mass desire your market already has?
Write it as a single sentence in the prospect's own words. Not "save time" — but "I don't want to spend Saturday at the office anymore."

### Question 2 — How aware is your prospect, and how sophisticated is the market?
See `schwartz-awareness-levels` — the 5 awareness levels and 5 sophistication stages determine hook type, copy length, and angle.

### Question 3 — What can your product's performance actually do?
List every concrete benefit and result the product produces, with specifics. Not "makes you more productive" — but "saves 1.4 hours per day on email triage (measured in 47 customers)."

**Write copy only after all three questions are answered.** Most bad copy skips all three.

## Part 3 — Performance claim vs mechanism claim

Two types of claims you can make. The right type depends on market sophistication.

### Performance claim
**What the product does for the prospect.**
- "Lose 20 pounds in 30 days"
- "3× your open rate"
- "Get your first 10 clients in 60 days"

Performance claims work when:
- Market sophistication 1-2 (fresh or early)
- Claim is genuinely unique in the market
- Specific and measurable

### Mechanism claim
**How the product achieves the result — the process, system, ingredient, method.**
- "Reset your insulin response with a 4-step protocol"
- "Use the hidden RFM audience layer inside Meta's ad platform"
- "Apply the 5-stage EA walk-forward validation system"

Mechanism claims work when:
- Market sophistication 3-4 (claims are tired, but the *why* is new)
- You have a genuinely unique process
- You can name it memorably (see Part 6)

**Schwartz's progression**: As a market matures, performance claims stop working → you switch to mechanism claims → the mechanism also matures → you switch to new mechanism or identity (Stage 5).

### New mechanism vs better mechanism
Don't say your mechanism is "better" than competitors. Say it's **different at a structural level** — a new category entirely.

- ❌ "A better indicator for trading Gold"
- ✅ "Not an indicator at all — a volatility regime classifier that knows when indicators don't work"

## Part 4 — The 6 fundamental ways to sell

Schwartz identified 6 selling angles. Every piece of copy uses at least one, usually a combination:

### 1. Promise + Proof
Make a specific performance claim and back it with evidence.
- "Lose 20 lbs in 30 days. Here are 47 before/after photos."
- Works at low sophistication or when proof is overwhelming.

### 2. Identification
Mirror the reader's exact situation so completely they feel seen.
- "If you're a founder over 30 running a $1M–$5M business, tired of doing everything yourself, exhausted by the hustle that got you here but isn't getting you to the next level — this is for you."
- Works at any sophistication; essential at Stage 5.

### 3. Secret
Reveal hidden or proprietary knowledge.
- "The 3-word email script that Sam Altman uses to reply to investors"
- "The one setting in Xcode that reduces SwiftUI compile times by 60%"
- Works at sophistication 2-4. Loses power at Stage 5 (secrets are exhausted).

### 4. Gossip
Use curiosity about someone else's story.
- "The 28-year-old who built a $400K/month coaching business from her parents' garage — and the weird principle she copied from poker"
- Works at all stages. Taps into deep voyeurism/social learning wiring.

### 5. Social Proof / Bandwagon
Show that similar others are already doing it.
- "The framework now used by 8 of the top 10 VC-backed SaaS companies"
- "473 founders in the Founders Club last quarter"
- Works at sophistication 2+ (requires actual proof points).

### 6. Unique Mechanism
Sell *how* the product works differently.
- "Not a course. Not coaching. The first software that writes 80% of your sales copy in your brand voice automatically."
- The most powerful move in a saturated market.

**Schwartz's rule**: pick one primary angle, support with 1-2 secondary angles. Five angles = no angle.

## Part 5 — The 36 headline formulas

Schwartz catalogued headline structures that repeatedly convert. The full list from *Breakthrough Advertising*:

### How-to / Instructional
1. "How to ___"
2. "How [famous person] ___"
3. "How I ___"
4. "How to ___ in ___ days"
5. "How to ___ without ___"

### Question
6. "Do you ___?"
7. "Are you ___?"
8. "Who else wants ___?"
9. "Can you ___?"
10. "What would you do if ___?"

### Reason-why / List
11. "X reasons why ___"
12. "X ways to ___"
13. "X things every ___ should know"
14. "X secrets of ___"
15. "X mistakes ___ make"

### Warning / Fear
16. "Warning: ___"
17. "Don't ___ until you read this"
18. "Beware of ___"
19. "The #1 mistake ___ are making"

### Curiosity / Secret
20. "The secret of ___"
21. "What nobody tells you about ___"
22. "The truth about ___"
23. "Little-known ways to ___"

### Promise
24. "Get ___ in ___"
25. "Finally, ___"
26. "At last, ___"
27. "Now you can ___"
28. "Imagine ___"

### Testimonial / Story
29. "They laughed when I ___, but then I ___"
30. "How a ___ became ___"
31. "From ___ to ___ in ___"

### Demonstration / Offer
32. "Free ___"
33. "If you ___, you can ___"
34. "New ___"
35. "Introducing ___"
36. "Announcing ___"

**Usage rules:**
- Use these as **starting points**, not finished headlines.
- Each structure works for a specific awareness level (see `schwartz-awareness-levels`).
- Test at least 3 formulas per piece of copy.
- The best headline wins on conversion, not aesthetics.

## Part 6 — The Uniqueness Claim (USP on steroids)

Schwartz insisted every product needs one and only one **Uniqueness Claim** — the one sentence that positions you as different from every competitor in a way prospects care about.

### Uniqueness Claim template
> "We are the only [category] that [unique mechanism] for [specific audience] so they can [specific outcome] without [common pain/trade-off]."

**Example:**
> "We are the only MT5 Gold trading system that uses ATR-adaptive position sizing with news-event blackout gating, for retail traders with $10k+ accounts, so they can survive 6-month drawdowns without blowing their accounts or touching martingale."

### Uniqueness audit
1. Is "only" literally true? (Or at least defensible?)
2. Is the category clear?
3. Is the mechanism specific, not vague?
4. Is the audience narrow enough that they feel "that's me"?
5. Is the outcome concrete?
6. Is the trade-off they're avoiding the one they actually fear?

If you can't pass this audit, you don't have a uniqueness claim — you have a generic marketing page.

## Part 7 — Schwartz's working process

From interviews and his own writing:

1. **Research obsessively.** Schwartz would read every competitor's ad, every trade magazine, every customer letter, until he could *think* like the prospect. He said a good ad came from 90% research + 10% writing.

2. **Write everything down.** He kept lists of phrases, headlines, objections, proof points — a "swipe file" decades before anyone named it.

3. **Timer method.** Schwartz worked in 33-minute sprints with zero distraction, 3 sprints per morning. Then he was done for the day.

4. **Rewrite until you strip every unnecessary word.** "Good copy is short. Great copy is half as short."

5. **Read it out loud.** If you stumble, the reader will too.

6. **Test in market, not in committee.** A winning headline lifts conversion 2-5×. Nothing committee-designed does this.

## Part 8 — The 4 levels of market sophistication (deep dive)

This extends `schwartz-awareness-levels` Part 2 with deeper tactical moves:

### Stage 1 — Make the claim
Market is fresh. State the promise directly and confidently.
- "Lose weight without dieting."
- "Build a million-dollar SaaS in a year."
- One clear benefit. No explanation needed.

### Stage 2 — Enlarge the claim
Competitors copied you. Make the claim bigger or more specific.
- "Lose 20 pounds in 30 days without giving up the foods you love."
- Add specifics, timeframes, caveats that increase credibility while enlarging the promise.

### Stage 3 — The mechanism
Claims are tired. Introduce how it works.
- "Reset your insulin sensitivity using a 4-step morning protocol."
- The mechanism is the product now. The benefit is secondary.

### Stage 4 — Elaborate the mechanism
Mechanisms are tired. Make yours novel.
- "Not glucose. Not insulin. It's your gut microbiome, and a new class of prebiotics finally targets the specific strain that controls weight."
- You sound like a scientist. That's the point.

### Stage 5 — Identification
Everything's been said. Sell to who they are.
- "For the 40-year-old who refused to do 'just eat less' — and found something that respects your body."
- Identity > mechanism > benefit at this stage.

### Cross-stage rule
**Read your market like a history book.** What claims were made 10 years ago? 5 years ago? Last year? This year? Your sophistication stage is **one level ahead of the last working claim**.

## Part 9 — Schwartz's copywriting laws

1. **"The market always wins."** Your clever copy doesn't beat an existing mass desire. Find the desire, then write.
2. **"The headline does 80% of the work."** Spend 80% of time on headlines.
3. **"The reader is the hero. The product is a tool."** Never the other way around.
4. **"Prove everything. Assume nothing."** Every claim needs specific proof attached.
5. **"Don't sell the steak. Sell the sizzle."** Sell the experience of having it, not the spec sheet.
6. **"Price is always secondary to value."** If you're competing on price, you're not competing at all.
7. **"Write to one person, not 'the market'."** One specific human. Imagine them. Address them.
8. **"Every word must earn its place."** Strip until nothing more can be cut without losing meaning.

## Part 10 — Quick audit (Schwartz-level)

Before publishing any piece of paid marketing copy:

- [ ] Have you named the mass desire in one concrete sentence in the prospect's words?
- [ ] Is the awareness level identified (Level 1-5)?
- [ ] Is the market sophistication stage identified (Stage 1-5)?
- [ ] Does the hook match the awareness level?
- [ ] Does the angle (performance or mechanism) match the sophistication stage?
- [ ] Is there a single primary selling angle (one of the 6)?
- [ ] Does every claim have proof attached?
- [ ] Is there a single clear Uniqueness Claim?
- [ ] Would the reader feel "that's me" in the first 3 lines?
- [ ] Have you read it out loud without stumbling?
- [ ] Can you cut 30% more words?
- [ ] Does it end with a specific offer, not a generic "learn more"?
